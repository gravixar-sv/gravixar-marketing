Gravixar is the software I build to run a business's daily operations. AI does the busywork, and a person approves anything that spends money, changes a record, or goes out the door. You see it running before you buy it.

## How I work

Every system I build follows that one rule. When a team needs it shaped around how they work, I build it myself. There is no agency layer between you and the person doing the work. That's me.

The systems I build for clients are live and running, and I usually run a version myself before I recommend one. There are five demo apps at [demo.gravixar.com](https://demo.gravixar.com): sample data, no sign-in, nothing to install. They are built separately from any client's system, on purpose. Pick the one closest to your work, click through it, and decide if it makes sense before you ever get on a call.

I build in small steps. In week one you have something running, even if it is small. By week ten you have something that has been used in real conditions and rounded off where the corners caught. I don't disappear into a four-month build and come back with a "v1" you have never seen.

Running four live client projects at once only works because the setup behind them is a system too, with checks that run every day and tell me when something has drifted. This is what it carries.

The market scan hands me a ranked list and waits. Nothing on it acts by itself. That is what I sell: AI handles the boring 80%, and a person keeps the steering wheel.

## Background

I started managing operations in 2009 and have been doing it in some form ever since: HubSpot, monday.com, ClickUp, Zoho, and most of the rest of the suite over the years, on teams that were on-site, remote, and everything in between. These days I also head operations for an Islamabad-based creative agency on top of my own work, so the patterns I sell aren't things I've read about. They're things I run.

I've watched enough teams stitch Notion, Sheets and a Slack channel into something that almost works to know that "almost" is where most of the cost lives. The gap between that and a real system is where I tend to be useful.

## A few things I'm not

- Not a CRM developer, though I've built portals with CRM-shaped pieces in them.
- Not a vendor who'll tell you AI is the answer to a problem AI is bad at.
- Not a black box. Every system I ship comes with a record of what it did, the code itself, and the reasons behind each decision. I have written down 493 decisions and 71 infrastructure notes so far, and I only ever add to them, so nothing gets quietly rewritten later.
