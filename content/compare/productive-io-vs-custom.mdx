## The honest version

Most agency comparisons I read are written by people selling one side or the other. I'll declare my bias: I build custom client portals. I also send agencies to Productive.io regularly, because for a real subset of teams it's the right call. The trick is knowing which side you're on before you spend a year fighting your tool.

Productive.io's strongest pitch is for the agency that has standardized their shape. Five-person studios trying to figure out their delivery model don't fit. Forty-person agencies that have run the same playbook for three years fit beautifully. The middle (roughly 12 to 25 people, scaling, still iterating on how delivery works) is where the friction lives.

## Where Productive.io wins

The time-tracking and utilization reporting. This is hard to beat. If your headline metric is billable utilization across an entire team and you need the multi-currency, multi-entity slice, building this from scratch is wasteful. Use the tool.

Their integration story for accounting (QuickBooks, Xero) is mature. If your finance team lives in those tools, Productive.io is the cheapest path to clean monthly reporting.

Their support is responsive. Not flashy, just present.

## Where Productive.io breaks

The client portal is the part most agencies eventually outgrow. It's serviceable. It's not the part Productive.io is investing in. Once what your clients see matters (a proper sign-off flow, your own branding, AI helping with new inquiries, a record of who changed what), you're either layering Notion on top or you're rebuilding what you wanted in the first place.

The workflow is opinionated. If your delivery shape doesn't line up with theirs (and most agencies under 25 people don't), you spend a quarter teaching the team the tool, then another quarter writing Notion docs to cover the gaps. The Notion docs are the tell. Notion docs about your tool mean your tool isn't the tool.

Custom workflows live in their "Automations" area, which works for simple if-this-then-that rules and chokes on anything with several steps. Change history is kept under one rule, whatever the record is about. There's no real story for AI in the loop beyond a chrome extension that summarizes a project page.

## What "custom portal" actually means

I have a working example of the alternative. [The agency portal I built for a 4-year client](/work/bs-hub) runs their full delivery flow: every inquiry moves through 12 fixed steps, every deliverable goes through sign-off, changes to contracts and money are kept for seven years, an AI intake drafts a brand brief from each prospect's website, and a nightly check emails the admins when something looks wrong. If you want to see the moving parts before signing anything, [demo.gravixar.com](https://demo.gravixar.com) has five purpose-built scenes with no login and no sales call in front of them. Those scenes are their own build, not a copy of a client's production system, because live client data does not belong in a sales demo.

The recipe is documented and ready to reuse, and the honest measure of it is the code. A recount on 21 July 2026 counted 84 database models, 255 endpoints and 9 scheduled jobs. When I spin up a portal for the next agency I work down that surface and decide what to keep, drop, or customize. A pure task tracker keeps a thin slice. An ops-heavy firm keeps most of the finance and audit layers. The unit of work is a slice of something already running, not the rebuild.

## How to decide

Run this in order:

1. **List the parts of your delivery your client actually sees.** If they're hidden (your team uses Productive.io, your client gets a PDF), Productive.io is fine. If they're visible (the client logs into a portal, reviews work, signs off), the off-the-shelf experience matters and Productive.io probably loses.
2. **Count seats, then be honest about which way the number points.** It points at Productive.io. At $25 a seat on Professional, their licence is cheaper than my retainer at every headcount an agency in this range has, and it is not close. Seats are an argument for the tool. What actually moves with headcount is the cost of working around it: the hours your team loses in the gaps, and the Notion docs in step 3. Count those first, and if they come to nothing, buy the licence.
3. **Audit your Notion docs about your tool.** If you have any, your tool isn't the tool. That's a strong signal for custom.
4. **Ask if AI assist is going to live inside or outside your workflow.** If outside (an add-on that summarizes a page), Productive.io is fine. If inside (an intake that asks follow-up questions, briefs drafted for you, a nightly check for anything unusual), custom is the only path that doesn't fight the tool.
