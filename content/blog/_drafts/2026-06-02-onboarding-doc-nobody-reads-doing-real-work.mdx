## The complaint I hear most

Every small-team operator I talk to has the same story. They wrote a detailed onboarding doc. New contractor arrived, skimmed it for thirty seconds, asked the exact questions the doc answered, then proceeded to do things their own way anyway.

The conclusion most people draw: onboarding docs are a waste of time. Just hop on a call and walk them through it.

I drew the same conclusion for about two years. I was wrong.

## What the doc is actually for

The onboarding doc is not primarily a communication tool. It is a forcing function.

When I sit down to write one, I have to answer questions I have been avoiding. What does "done" mean for a deliverable on this project? Who approves scope changes? What counts as a blocker that requires immediate escalation versus a note in the weekly update?

These are not questions I can leave vague when I am writing sentences. Vague prose reveals itself immediately. "Communicate proactively" means nothing. "If a deliverable will be more than 24 hours late, send a message in Slack before end of day" means something.

The act of writing forces specificity. The contractor reading it is almost secondary.

## The decisions that surface

I maintain a portal for ongoing client delivery work. When I onboard someone new to support a project in that system, writing their onboarding doc reliably surfaces three or four things I had not made explicit:

**Access boundaries.** Which views can they see? Which can they edit? I think I know the answer until I have to write it down. Then I realize I never actually decided whether contractors should see the client's internal notes column, or just the status column. That is not a onboarding doc problem. That is a permissions architecture problem I was ignoring.

**Escalation path.** Who do they contact when the client goes out of scope? I assumed this was obvious. It is not. Writing it out reveals that I had two different answers depending on which part of the project was affected, and I had never reconciled them.

**Tool ownership.** When a shared doc needs updating, who owns the update? I have seen projects stall for a week because a contractor assumed I would update a tracker, and I assumed they would. The onboarding doc is the right place to kill that ambiguity before it becomes a delay.

None of these issues are solved by the contractor reading the doc. They are solved by me writing it.

## Why the doc still gets ignored

I want to be honest about what the doc does not fix. If a contractor skips it, you will still have those calls. You will still answer repeat questions. The doc does not change human behavior reliably, especially for contractors who have their own established workflows.

What I do now: the doc is not a prerequisite I expect them to memorize. It is a reference I point back to when something goes wrong. "The escalation path for this is in the onboarding doc, section three" is a reasonable response the first time. The second time it is a signal that the working relationship needs a direct conversation.

The doc also serves as a changelog for my own thinking. When I update it after a project goes sideways, I am documenting the decision I made to prevent that specific failure. Twelve months later, when I onboard someone to a similar project, I am not starting from scratch.

## The format that actually works

I stopped using long prose docs. I use a short structured template: four sections, each capped at one screen of text.

1. What this project is (two sentences, no history)
2. How work flows (the steps, with tool names and expected turnaround times)
3. Escalation and approval (named people, not roles)
4. What I care most about (one or two non-obvious things specific to this client or project)

Section four is where I put the things I would tell them on a call but never write down otherwise. On one project it was: "The client reads every status update closely and will reply to anything that sounds uncertain. Keep status language factual." On another: "Invoices submitted after the 25th will not process until the following month, no exceptions."

That section takes me fifteen minutes to write and saves at least one uncomfortable conversation.

## The discipline I am still building

I do not write the doc every time. When I am moving fast and onboarding someone for a short engagement, I skip it and tell myself the project is too simple to need one. This is usually wrong. The projects I consider simple are often the ones where implicit assumptions are densest, because I have been running them long enough that nothing feels like a decision anymore.

The discipline I am working on is writing the doc regardless of engagement length. Not because every contractor will read it carefully. Because every time I write it, I find something I had stopped seeing.
