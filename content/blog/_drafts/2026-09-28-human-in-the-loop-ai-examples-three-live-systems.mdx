"Human in the loop" has become a phrase people put on a slide. It usually means somebody could look at the output if they wanted to. That is not a loop. That is a hope.

I run three systems where a person really does sit between the AI and the result. They are built differently, because the risk in each one is different. But I judge all three with the same two questions, and I think they are the only two that matter.

**Can the work move forward without the person?** If it can, the person is optional, and optional steps get skipped in a busy week.

**Can I count the times the person said no?** If every review ends in "approved", I have a rubber stamp, not a reviewer.

Here is how each system answers them.

## Example 1: an agency portal where AI drafts and never approves

The first is a client portal for a digital agency. AI works at three points in it: it writes a brand brief from a new client's website, it writes the follow-up questions in the signup form, and it runs a daily check for anything unusual in the activity log.

None of those three can approve anything. Every deliverable moves through five fixed steps: draft, sent for internal review, approved internally, sent to the client, approved by the client. Each step is a button a named person presses, and each press writes a line to the audit log. There is no free-text status field an admin can quietly edit to "approved". The [full build is written up here](/work/bs-hub).

The part I would copy into any system is smaller. The activity log has a Restore button that undoes a change in one click, but only for fields on a short approved list. Status, money amounts, and who owns a task are never on it. Those need a person to decide, every time, and the list is where that line is drawn in the code.

It also works the other way. If the AI is down or slow, its step is skipped and everything else goes ahead. The person is required. The AI is not.

## Example 2: a healthcare platform where the check runs before the person

The second is a credentialing platform for a healthcare billing company. The risk there is not a bad draft. It is patient data ending up somewhere it should not.

So the order is reversed. A machine check runs before any person sees anything: every free-text box is checked for patient details before it is saved, and every AI request carries a standing instruction to keep them out. Patient data does not live in the system at all, and the code enforces that, not a policy document.

This is the honest exception to "keep a human in the loop". When the rule is mechanical, like "no patient identifiers in this field", a person is the wrong check. People get tired. A check in the code does not. I put people where the call needs judgement, and code where it needs to be the same every time. The [platform is written up here](/work/beeline).

## Example 3: this blog, where I can count the no's

The third is the one you are reading. An AI drafts posts for this site, and every draft lands in a folder the site does not publish. A post goes live only when I move the file out of that folder, which is a change in the code history with my name and a date on it. I wrote about why that queue exists in [the bottleneck is approval, not generation](/blog/bottleneck-is-approval-not-generation).

That setup passes the first test by construction. The second test is the interesting one, because I can answer it from the history.

Since the end of May, 20 drafts have reached that folder. I published 14 of them, usually within about four days. I deleted the other 6, and two of those I deleted rather than tried to fix.

The deletions have a pattern. The drafts I published were about one specific mechanism: a status log, an approval step, a portal that broke at a certain size. Most of the ones I deleted were general advice anyone could have written, like how to write an onboarding document. That tells me what the review is for. It is not catching typos. It is catching posts that should not exist.

A reviewer who deleted nothing would have told me the opposite: either the AI was perfect, or nobody was really reading.

## How to check your own system

If you have AI in your workflow and someone tells you a person reviews it, ask three things.

- **Show me where it stops.** Find the step the AI cannot pass on its own. If the answer is "the team knows to check", there is no step.
- **Show me a no.** Ask for a rejected output from the last month, with who rejected it and why. No rejections means no review.
- **Show me what the person never touches.** Some checks should be code, not people. If a human is the only thing standing between your client data and an AI prompt, move that check into the system.

Most teams I talk to can answer the first question and not the second. That is the gap worth closing, and it is the part I build into [the AI systems I set up for clients](/services/ai-tooling).
