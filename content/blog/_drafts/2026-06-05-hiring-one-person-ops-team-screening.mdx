## The Problem With Most Ops Job Posts

Most ops job posts are written by people who do not do ops work. They list software tools as requirements. They ask for "experience managing stakeholders." They want someone who is "comfortable with ambiguity" — a phrase that means nothing and signals nothing.

I have posted, screened, and hired for ops roles in lean environments. The thing that kills a hire is not a skill gap. It is a mismatch in how someone thinks about incomplete information. Ops work, especially at the one- or two-person scale, is 80% judgment and 20% execution. You can teach Notion. You cannot teach someone to notice when a process has stopped working.

## What I Screen For Before Any Interview

I ask for a short written response to one scenario question before I schedule anything. Not a cover letter. A specific scenario.

The question I use looks like this: "A client deliverable is due Friday. On Wednesday you realize the person responsible has not logged any progress and is not responding to messages. Walk me through what you do."

I am not looking for the correct answer. There is no single correct answer. I am reading for three things:

- Do they escalate immediately, or do they try to solve it themselves first? Either can be right depending on context, but I want to see them acknowledge the trade-off.
- Do they mention communication with the client, or only internal actions? Someone who never mentions the client in this scenario will do the same thing on the job.
- Is their response specific, or is it a list of vague steps? Phrases like "I would reach out to the relevant parties" are disqualifying. The relevant parties are named. Name them.

This step removes about half the applicant pool. The people who remain write responses that read like they have actually been in that situation.

## The One Interview Question That Tells Me the Most

During the interview, I ask: "Tell me about a process you built that you later had to throw away."

This question does a few things. It distinguishes people who have built real systems from people who have managed existing ones. It also tells me how someone handles being wrong. In ops, you will build something that seems smart and then discover it creates more friction than it solves. The ability to scrap it without attachment is not common.

Red flags in responses:
- They struggle to name anything they built from scratch.
- They describe throwing something away because "leadership changed direction" — meaning they are placing all agency outside themselves.
- They describe the replacement as obviously better in every way. Real trade-offs exist. The replacement is always better on some dimensions and worse on others.

The best responses I have heard involve specific tools, specific timelines, and a honest account of what the person got wrong in their initial assumptions.

## Why I Do Not Screen on Tool Experience

I stopped listing specific tools as requirements in 2021. Here is what I found: people who list five project management platforms in their resume often know the surface of each and the depth of none. More importantly, the tools I use change. I have migrated client workflows three times in four years. Someone who is deeply attached to one platform becomes a liability when that platform's pricing model breaks or its API support degrades.

What I actually care about: can they learn a new tool in under a week and set it up correctly without hand-holding? I test this during the paid trial period, not the interview. I give them a real, scoped task in whatever tool is live and ask them to document what they did.

## The Paid Trial Is Not Optional

For any ops hire, I run a paid two-to-three week trial before any longer commitment. This is not controversial to me. The cost of a three-week trial is low. The cost of a six-month hire who does not work out is high — not just in money, but in the cleanup. Ops mistakes compound. A bad intake process or a broken client status workflow does not announce itself loudly. It leaks quietly for months.

During the trial I give the person one system to own end-to-end. Not help with — own. That means they are the one who notices when it breaks and the one who proposes the fix. I observe whether they do that without being prompted. Proactive system ownership is the whole job. If I have to prompt it during the trial, it will not improve once they are full-time.

## The Honest Trade-Off in Keeping a Role Open

There is real pressure to fill an ops role fast when things are breaking. I have felt it. The mistake is lowering the bar because you are in pain. The hire you make under pressure is rarely the hire you would make at a measured pace, and the person often senses it — they know they were the best available, not the best fit.

I have kept roles open for ten to twelve weeks before. That is uncomfortable. But the ops work that piled up during those weeks was recoverable. The trust damage from a bad hire in a delivery-facing role takes much longer to fix.

The standard I hold: if I would not trust this person to run a client status call without me in the room after four weeks, I do not make the offer.

## What This Means in Practice

The screening process I use is not sophisticated. It is:

1. A written scenario question before any call.
2. One interview focused on past systems they built and abandoned.
3. A paid trial with a scoped, real task and clear ownership expectations.

That is it. No personality assessments. No panel interviews with three stakeholders who each want different things. No skills matrices.

Ops hiring fails when it tries to be comprehensive before it is concrete. Know what judgment you need. Test for that judgment directly. The rest follows.
