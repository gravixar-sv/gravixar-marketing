## What I built

The portal handles four distinct surfaces in one codebase: **public marketing and the signup wizard**, **a client's view of their own work**, **the operations console for project managers and admins**, and **internal HR and presence tools**. Each has its own permissions, its own dashboard, and its own record of who changed what.

Every named unit, from sign-in at the base to the daily security check at the top, is catalogued in the repository with a map of what depends on what. That catalogue is the recipe. When I build a portal for the next client, I work down the list and decide unit by unit what to keep, drop, or change. A plain task tracker needs a fraction of it. An internal HR tool needs less than that.

The piece I'm proudest of is the review flow on tasks. Every deliverable moves through five fixed steps:

Draft → sent for internal review → approved internally → sent to the client → approved by the client.

A revision request from the client sends it back into the loop. Each step does one job: it sends its emails and notifications and writes a line to the audit log. No step is implied. No admin can flip a task to "approved" by editing a free-text field.

## Decisions I'd defend

**The fixed steps are non-negotiable.** Free-text status fields are how "almost works" turns into "definitely doesn't." Every step has explicit conditions and side effects, and every one writes an audit row. That discipline pays for itself the first time a client says a deliverable was never approved.

**Records are kept on two clocks.** Anything financial or contractual is kept for 7 years. Everyday changes are kept for 1 year. The rule for each kind of record is written into the code, and arguments about what to keep stop once it is.

**When the AI fails, the work carries on.** Every AI step, the brand brief, the daily warning check, the intake question writer, has a fallback. If the AI cannot be reached or takes too long, that part is skipped and everything else goes ahead. I will not ship a system where the core work waits on someone else's model.

**Only some changes can be undone in one click.** The activity log has a Restore button on changed rows, but only for fields on a short approved list. Status, money amounts and who owns a task are never on it. Those need a human decision, and the list is where that line is drawn.

**Every finding has to survive an attempt to disprove it.** On 29 July 2026, 14 agents working in parallel hunted for problems in the code, then a second pass tried to knock every finding down. All 63 findings survived, and 11 came back with a corrected severity. Two rounds of fixes followed. They closed all 63, plus older issues they pulled in along the way, 87 in total, and every test passed afterwards, 252 of 252. The second pass is the part that matters: it keeps an audit from becoming a list of things that merely sounded bad.

## What broke

**Activation was slow on big retainers.** When a project manager activates a heavy retainer, the portal creates milestones, tasks, and an AI brief for each task. Picking the right person for each task by skill and workload takes about 5 database lookups. On a 30-deliverable retainer that's 150 lookups in a row before activation finishes. The first version timed out. The fix was to run activation in the background and show its progress on screen. Meanwhile the button reads "Activating… (spinning up tasks)" and stays disabled, so nobody double-clicks during the 70 seconds.

**The skip-payment coupon was supposed to be temporary.** I added a coupon that skips payment, to cover the demo period before live Stripe payments were switched on. It's still there, and it will probably stay as a way to give free access and run demos. Some temporary hacks earn their keep.

**Getting "Lock scope" right took two rounds.** Project managers submitted call notes with a "Mark as Call Done" button, which then locked the scope. Users pushed back: the words didn't match the action. They were locking a scope, not just closing a call. I renamed it "Lock Scope" and added "Unlock & re-scope" for the assigned project manager. Sounds trivial. It meant a lot of email-template editing, because the lock sets off a notification, a dashboard banner, and a chain of emails.

**The first cancellation deleted everything.** Wrong. Cancelling needs to keep the record, remove the tasks, put the inquiry back where it can be re-scoped, and issue a credit. That took a rebuild. Credits are now records of their own, and the planned finance module will treat them that way.
